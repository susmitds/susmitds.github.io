I was building stuff before the LLM thing.

The hardware has changed. The basic impulse has not.

First: combat robots.

During my CS undergrad I spent a frankly disproportionate amount of time designing, fabricating and competing with combat robots at events around India. It was messy, mechanical, electrical, occasionally destructive, and extremely fun.

It also taught me a durable lesson: a design that sounds clever on paper still has to survive contact with the arena.

Now: unnecessarily large models at home.

I run a mixed four-GPU workstation because I genuinely enjoy making new open-weight models work locally. That means patching inference engines when an architecture is awkward on my setup, porting low-bit quantization paths, messing with kernels, writing an MoE expert-mix optimizer, and generally refusing to accept “you probably need a server for that” as the end of the conversation.

I use the setup for my own coding agents and experiments. The hands-on systems work transfers nicely into research, but most of it exists for the much simpler reason that I think this technology is ridiculously interesting.

Current unsolved systems problem: cable management.

And then there’s the day job.

Useful reality checks are underrated.

I work on privacy-focused AI systems at PrivaSapien, where I’ve gone from intern to Privacy Research Scientist (AI). The work has included multi-agent systems, document-grounded assistants, privacy-preserving inference, privacy threat modeling, research, mentoring, and conversations with people who actually have to use the systems we build.

I like that part of the job: technical ideas have to survive actual requirements, messy documents, changing user inputs, governance constraints, and people who quite reasonably do not care how elegant the architecture looked in a diagram.
